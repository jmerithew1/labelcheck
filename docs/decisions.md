# Decisions — append-only, newest at top. Every entry names the rejected alternative.

## 2026-08-14 (batch check) — the fallback claimed failure before it had looked

**Asked to check the batch page for the same three issues, and the check found one I had just introduced there.** The batch detail panel renders the same `ResultView`, so the always-listed warning row and the whole-row click carried over for free — but the foot-of-label fallback did not, because the two pages learn where the warning is at different times.

The single-check page gets its bands with the verdict. The batch panel fetches them **lazily** when a row opens. So on batch, opening a row and clicking the warning immediately produced "Couldn't pinpoint the government warning on this image" — and then corrected itself to the normal caption a few seconds later when `/api/locate` returned. The app was announcing a failed search before the search had started.

That is precisely the dishonesty the bold gate's `measuring` state exists to prevent, reappearing in a new place, and it was mine, hours old. `ResultView` now takes `bandsPending`, and only guesses once the search has actually finished and come up empty; the batch passes `r.bands === undefined` (never fetched) rather than `{}` (fetched, nothing found) — a distinction the batch code already maintained and the viewer was ignoring. The single page gets the same protection while its OCR repair is still running. **Rejected**: showing a "looking for the warning…" state during the wait, which puts a spinner in front of a person for a sub-second gap they would otherwise never notice; and dropping the fallback on the batch, which would leave the same dead click the fallback was written to fix.

Verified on the batch panel: clicking the warning immediately after opening a row now shows the normal caption and no false claim, and once the locator lands the row draws its box. Both standing harnesses re-run clean (`single-check-upload` 7/7, `round-trip-batch` 4/4).

**Worth noting what this run also showed about the locator.** The same vodka label had NO warning band through `/api/check` on the single page and a good one through `/api/locate` on the batch panel, minutes apart. Two calls, one image, different answers — which is the band-accuracy finding from earlier today showing up again, and the reason nothing downstream is allowed to trust a band without a repair path behind it.

## 2026-08-14 (warning row) — the same subject in two different shapes, and a click that did nothing

**Owner compared the "Warning issue" and "Multiple issues" samples and asked why their government warnings behaved differently.** Two causes, and only one of them was intentional.

**The comparison list carried a Government warning row only when the warning failed or could not be read.** So a failing warning appeared twice (list + panel) and a passing one appeared once (panel only) — the same subject in a different shape depending on the verdict, which is exactly the sort of thing that makes a reader stop and work out whether something is missing. The intent was sound (promote problems into the list a person scans) and the effect was not. It is now listed every time, PASS / Review / Fail, matching the panel; the panel remains where the two halves, wording and formatting, are split out. **Rejected**: removing it from the list entirely and letting the panel own it — the warning is the one check that can hard-fail, so it belongs in the list a person scans; and leaving it conditional, which is the behaviour that prompted the question.

**Clicking that row did nothing at all on one of the two samples**, while still showing a pointer cursor under a caption that says "click any row to see where it sits on the label". Confirmed at the API rather than guessed: for `batch-vodka--shadow2` the locator returns bands for brand, class, ABV and net contents and **no warning band**, and the client's OCR repair cannot find it either — the shadow across the foot of that label defeats both. Measured from a clean state: brand click drew one box, warning click drew zero. A dead affordance under an instruction to use it.

The viewer now falls back to the foot of the label — where the mandated statement sits on essentially every submission — and the caption changes to say it is a guess. That is the same honesty the batch spot-check card has carried since yesterday; this path simply never got it. **Rejected**: making the row un-clickable when no band exists, which removes the dead click but leaves the person with nothing to look at and no explanation; and drawing the fallback silently, which is the wrong-place highlight the label viewer has always refused to draw.

**The single-check audit trail moved inside the result card.** It was rendered after `ResultView`, which put it below "Check another label" and outside the card entirely — far enough out of the main window that the owner nearly reported it missing. `ResultView` now takes it as a slot and renders it above the action row. **Rejected**: leaving placement to the caller, which is how it ended up outside the card in the first place.

Verified on both samples: the passing warning lists as **Pass** and its click now draws the captioned fallback; the failing one still lists as **Fail**, still draws its real located band, and still shows the normal caption.

## 2026-08-14 (front door) — a harness that enters the way a person does

**Follow-on from the sample-CSV trap.** The bug was fixed the same hour; the interesting part was why nothing here could have caught it. Every batch harness constructs its own input — `new File([blob], name)` plus a synthetic drop — and constructs it *correctly*. That is the right way to prove the engine, and it structurally cannot prove the affordances, because the step where a user assembles their payload is the step being skipped.

`samples/tools/round-trip-batch.mjs` is the missing shape: app **output** to disk to app **input**. It downloads exactly what the links serve and feeds those files through the real picker (`setInputFiles`, the same path as choosing files by hand), then reads the DOM for what a person would see. Three scenarios — the spreadsheet alone must give one actionable message and zero rows; the zip must unpack and pair every label it carries; randomised CSVs drawn from arbitrary `samples/labels` sidecars must pair, run, and produce no false rejection on any label whose sidecar matches the canonical warning. **Rejected**: driving it at the API layer per row, which is cheaper and would have missed this defect entirely, since the guard that was absent lives in the UI; and asserting only on pairing, which would have passed a batch that paired and then failed every check.

**Randomised, not fixed, on purpose.** The shipped sample set is twelve labels chosen to demonstrate specific archetypes; a CSV built from a random draw of the 35 ground-truthed labels is a combination the repo has never shipped. The first draw pulled three defect labels and one clean one and got exactly that back (3 warning failures, 0 false rejections); the second pulled four clean ones including the case/punctuation label and passed all four. Seeded, so any failure is reproducible with `--seed`.

**Two of its own assertions were wrong on the first run**, and both are the same error this harness exists to catch. The "did it show an error" check matched the *dropzone's description copy* rather than the error banner — it would have gone green on a page showing no error at all. The row count read `tbody tr`, which is the paginated page (10), not the batch (12). Fixed to read the banner element and the "of N labels" footer, and the ground-truth predicate was tightened from "starts with GOVERNMENT WARNING:" to "equals the shipped canonical text", because the corpus is full of labels that open correctly and then drop a word — counting those as clean would have manufactured false "false rejections". **A harness that does not touch the claim is not evidence**, and that applies to the harness as much as to the app.

## 2026-08-14 (later still) — the sample CSV was a trap, reported from the outside

**Owner downloaded the sample CSV, dropped it into the batch page, and got an error on every row.** Reproduced exactly on production: twelve rows, each reading "No matching label file uploaded". The app was not wrong — it cannot check a label it does not have — but the page had invited the mistake and then answered it twelve times instead of once.

Two things were wrong, and only the second one is about copy.

**A spreadsheet with no labels at all built a row per application and failed each.** `onFiles` already handled the mirror case (labels with no spreadsheet) with a single clear sentence; the reverse had no equivalent, so it fell through to per-row pairing errors. Those errors are exactly right when SOME labels are missing — they name the CSV row and the file it wanted — and exactly the wrong instrument for "you brought no labels at all". That case now stops before any rows are built and says so in one sentence, naming the three ways forward (add the labels, load the sample batch, or take the zip). **Rejected**: leaving it, on the grounds that the per-row errors are technically accurate — accuracy that takes twelve red rows to say one thing is not usable error handling, which is a named evaluation criterion.

**The download was labelled by format instead of by contents.** "sample CSV" next to "Load the sample batch" reads as "the sample", so taking it and dropping it straight back in is the obvious move. The links now say what they carry: *sample bundle (zip — spreadsheet + labels)* first, *spreadsheet only* second. **Rejected**: removing the CSV download — it is the only artifact that documents the expected column format, and `buildRows` points at it by name when a column is missing.

**Verified by reproducing the report, not by reading the diff**: the CSV alone now yields one message and **zero rows** (was twelve error rows), while CSV + a partial set of labels still builds its rows and still reports a pairing issue per missing file — the behaviour that was right stayed right.

**Method note.** This was found by a person using the thing, not by any harness here — the 250-label runs, the real-label corpus and the out-of-sample batch all drove the dropzone with files already in hand, so none of them could ever have taken the download link and come back through the front door. Worth remembering what that class of test structurally cannot see.

## 2026-08-14 (later) — the single check gets an audit trail, the whole row gets the click, and a print bug nobody had reported

**Traceability existed on exactly one of the two surfaces.** The batch detail panel has carried an audit trail since it shipped; the single-check page had none. The same label was therefore fully traceable when it arrived in a batch and completely opaque when a person checked it by hand — which is every demo sample and every file a user uploads. For a tool whose pitch is "evidence-linked and audit-ready", that was the wrong half to be missing. `AuditTrail` moved out of `BatchReview` into its own component with a neutral prop shape, and the single page renders it collapsed at the foot of the result.

**Collapsed, not tabs and not always-open.** The single result is one calm scroll built for the "something my mother could figure out" bar, and the trail is investigation material — wanted when a verdict is disputed, noise on the other 95% of results. **Rejected**: tabs matching the batch panel, which would restructure a page already verified end to end and hide the evidence behind a click for the common case; and always-open, which lengthens every clean result to serve the rare disputed one.

**Fixing it surfaced a print bug in the batch that nobody had reported.** Print CSS only hides `.no-print`, and the trail was mounted only while its tab was active — so printing from *Overview* silently dropped the audit trail, and printing from *Audit trail* dropped the evidence. Whichever tab you were on, the filed copy was missing half the record. Both are now mounted and toggled with CSS, so print carries evidence *and* trail either way, with the on-screen tabs unchanged; the single page's collapsed trail is forced open in print for the same reason. **Rejected**: leaving print to follow the visible tab — a filed record that omits how the result was produced is precisely what an audit trail exists to prevent.

**The evidence rows were clickable only on their text** — measured at 73–75% of the row width, so clicking the status chip or the empty space did nothing and offered no reason why. The docs had just been corrected to say "click the wording", which is accurate but documents a limitation instead of removing it. The container now handles the click and ignores anything originating in a real control, so the row's own button and the Accept / Reject pair keep their own presses and nothing double-fires. **Rejected**: a stretched overlay button covering the row — it breaks text selection across the row and needs its own focus plumbing; and moving the decision controls inside the row button, which is invalid (a real button cannot nest inside another button) and fails 508. The inner button stays for semantics, keyboard operation and the accessible name.

**Application data stays typed by hand, and the reason is now stated rather than implied.** The brief rules out COLA integration, so there is no system of record a standalone prototype can read. Two things blunt it: a 200–300 label surge never types anything, because it arrives as the CSV the applications already export, so manual entry only ever applies to a one-off check; and paste-a-block parsing remains the top deferred item. **Rejected for this submission**: shipping paste parsing — a new input surface where every applicant formats their block differently, which needs its own test corpus to be trustworthy and trades against the brief's stated preference for a working core over ambitious incomplete features. **Rejected outright**: prefilling the application fields from the label's own reading, which demos well and is wrong — the tool would grade itself against its own transcription, destroying the only thing the comparison is for.

**Verified by exercising each control, not by reasoning about the diff**: clicking a status chip highlights (0 → 1 overlay), Accept and Reject record decisions without toggling the highlight, *Not provided* rows stay inert with a default cursor, keyboard focus and activation are unchanged, the print rules are present in the shipped stylesheet, and the batch panel holds the inactive tab's content in a print-only container in both directions.

## 2026-08-14 — the bold pass gets an OCR worker pool, and the measurement that justified it

**The batch had one part that did not scale, and it was found by measuring rather than by reasoning.** The check pass does 250 labels in ~140s. The opt-in bold pass over the same 250 managed about **four rows a minute** — every stroke-width measurement, plus the second full-image pass that repairs a wrong warning band, queued through a single tesseract worker.

**Fixed with a lazily-grown bounded pool** (`lib/pool.ts`, capped at `min(4, cores-1)`). Lazy growth is the point: a tesseract worker carries its own WASM heap and language data, so a single-label check must not pay for four. A new worker is created only when every existing one is busy and the cap has not been reached; past the cap, callers queue first-come-first-served. Both OCR entry points now acquire a worker BEFORE building their crop, which makes the pool bound peak memory as well as concurrency — on a 250-row batch every eligible row fires at once, and each crop is a canvas three times the width of the label.

**Extracted rather than inlined, because the logic deserved tests.** Six of them: sequential callers reuse one instance; concurrent demand grows to the cap and no further; waiters are served in order; a resource is handed straight to a waiter rather than parked; total creation failure returns null (which the gate already routes to a human glance); and a *partial* failure queues behind the healthy workers instead of failing a caller that could simply have waited.

**Evidence** ([ocr-pool.json](ocr-pool.json)). First, that the pool actually grows under the batch's real call pattern — `window.Worker` was patched with a counting proxy, giving 4 constructions with the pool and 1 when pinned. This mattered: the prior worry was that bands arrive one at a time and the pool would sit at size 1, and that worry was simply wrong. Then a controlled A/B — same machine, back to back, same 24-row batch, only `MAX_OCR_WORKERS` changed, with check passes 63.6s and 62.9s confirming equivalent conditions — cut the bold pass from **98s to 59s**. Then the size that matters: at 250 rows on the deployed app, **4 to 11.9 rows per minute, about 3x**, taking a surge batch from ten-plus minutes to roughly three and a half.

**Rejected alternatives.** A worker per row — tesseract workers are far too heavy, and 208 WASM heaps would take the tab down. An eager pool created at import — it would make every single-label check pay for four workers to serve one. Raising the cap past 4 — these run on government laptops and the crop preparation is main-thread canvas work, so leaving a core free is what keeps the UI answering. Loosening the gate's thresholds to reduce the number of rows needing measurement — that would trade a safety property for a speed one, which is the wrong direction on the one check that is a regulatory hard fail.

**What is deliberately not claimed:** that the pass is now fast. It is still client-side OCR and still minutes of background work at 250 rows. The honest production answer is to move the measurement server-side where it can run properly in parallel; the pool is the fix that fits a standalone prototype.

## 2026-08-13 (band accuracy) — what the locator is actually worth, and why nothing downstream trusts it

**The locator is a model estimate and it is wrong more often than the UI's "approximate location" caption implies.** Measured against the ground-truth boxes in `samples/labels/*.boxes.json`: on the 418x604 rosé every band came back compressed by a constant ~0.60 (brand 105-145 vs a true 174-282, warning 515-595 vs a true 854-919) — repeatably, and the same label at its native 720x1040 was accurate to within 4%. Rescaling the same pixels onto larger canvases tracked the error almost linearly (418px x0.60, 520px x0.75, 620px x0.89, 720px x0.95), and at 900px the warning came back missing altogether: the signature of pixel rows being reported where 0-1000 units were asked for, which only coincides when the image is about 1000px tall. Our own parser rejects anything over 1000, which is why the 1108px-tall gin's warning was dropped.

**The obvious fix does not survive contact with real labels, so it is not shipping.** Normalising every image to exactly 1000px tall fixed both synthetic cases perfectly. On 14 real approved TTB labels it made things WORSE — the band held the warning 8/14 as shipped and 6/14 normalised (`samples/tools/locate-band-audit.mjs`). A fix justified by two synthetic labels and refuted by fourteen real ones is not a fix. **Rejected alternative**: shipping it anyway on the strength of the mechanism being understood — the mechanism was understood and the prediction still failed, which is the whole reason for testing outside the sample set.

**What protects the product is that nothing downstream trusts the band.** Verified by forcing the failure rather than waiting for it — patching `fetch` to replace the warning band with the brand area, `[150,260]`, on a label whose real band that run was a useless `[990,1000]` sliver:

- the bold measurement finds no prefix in the crop, so `ocrWarningBand` reads the image for the real warning and measures THAT — the result came back green, "the prefix strokes measure heavier than the warning body"
- the label viewer's highlight followed the corrected band to 90.6% of the image height, where the warning actually is — not to the 15-26% band it was handed
- when OCR cannot find the warning either, the band is DROPPED rather than kept, so no highlight is drawn at all, and the batch's bold card falls back to the foot of the label with a caption saying it is guessing

So a wrong band costs a repair pass, not a wrong answer. The one thing it cannot do is silently point a human at the wrong part of a label and ask them to judge it.

## 2026-08-13 (out-of-sample round) — two defects that only a label we had never seen could find

**Every check up to here had been run against our own bundled samples.** Asked whether the app works on anything else, the answer was no, so a batch was built from real approved TTB back labels in `samples/real` — files the UI had never touched — with a CSV written the way an agent would write one: values typed from the application, in their own case and punctuation, plus one row whose file never made it into the folder. Both defects below were invisible to the sample set because our samples all carry brand, class, ABV and net contents.

**`/api/check` refused rows that plainly had application data.** The guard demanded one of the four CORE fields, ignoring bottler and country. Two of three rows errored with "the application fields are empty" on rows carrying a bottler name and address — and refusing the row also refused the GOVERNMENT WARNING check, which needs no application data at all. A back label's application row often carries little else, so on a real intake this throws away the one check that matters most. Any of the six fields now counts, and `SingleCheck`'s `canCheck` was making the same mistake in the form, so a page that looked ready to check produced a 400. **Rejected alternative**: allowing a check with no application data whatsoever — the warning check would run, but a form that submits empty is a worse default than one that asks for a single field.

**The bottler comparison was measuring the regulation, not the bottler.** 27 CFR 5.36 requires the name and address to be preceded by "BOTTLED BY" or similar; the application form carries the name alone. Whether that phrase reaches the comparison depends on how much of the line the reader returns, and it varies — on two real labels checked minutes apart, `BACK 40 LLC COLUMBIA, ILLINOIS` matched while `Bottled and Blended By Wise Ass Bottling Co. Bardstown, KY` was flagged at 59% similar against a bottler the agent had typed correctly. That is the brief's "surface, don't fail" line being crossed by boilerplate. The lead-in now comes off both sides before comparing, and only for comparing — the agent still sees the text as printed, and the note says the label also prints the required statement. Five tests pin it, including a genuinely different bottler that must still flag and a name beginning "Bottled Goods Inc" that must not be stripped. **Rejected alternative**: lowering the similarity threshold — it would have matched this pair and quietly matched real mismatches with it.

**What the same run confirmed, on labels never seen before:** case and punctuation judgment holds (`back 40 llc, columbia, illinois` matched `DISTILLED AND BOTTLED BY BACK 40 LLC / COLUMBIA, ILLINOIS`), a missing file is named by CSV row number and the batch waits for a human instead of auto-running, and the bold gate resolved on a 951x1350 label it had never measured.

## 2026-08-13 (deploy round) — the clean card's amber was a missing band, not a marginal measurement

**Deployed, then found on production what local runs had not.** The clean card came back amber on one run and green on the next. The first instinct — that the stroke measurement is marginal on a glare-washed photo — was wrong, and a new harness proved it wrong: `samples/tools/bold-gate-shipped-path.mjs` reproduces the SHIPPED path (call `/api/locate` N times to collect the bands the locator actually produces, crop each one exactly as `lib/boldMeasure.ts` does, run the real `applyBoldGate`). `clean-match--glare2` resolved **4/4** with `swRatio` 1.75-2.00 against a 1.225 threshold — the widest margin of any candidate measured, and wider than `batch-rye` (1.43), `batch-stout` (1.25), `stones-throw` (1.13) or `wine-label` (1.10). `clean-match--dark1`, the candidate that looked safest on paper, scored **0/4**: the locator returns a 5-per-mille sliver on the dark variant and OCR finds nothing in it.

**The actual cause was a gap in `SingleCheck`.** Its bold-gate effect bailed out whenever `bands.warning` was absent — and that band is a model output that is sometimes simply missing. With no band the effect never started, so the glance was declared owed on a label the machine had never looked at. The batch path has repaired exactly this case since it shipped (read the image for the warning, measure that); the single-check path never got the same treatment. It does now, and a genuine miss settles to `human` rather than leaving the headline stuck on "checking".

**Verified by forcing the condition, not by waiting for it.** Patching `fetch` to strip `bands.warning` from every `/api/check` response reproduces the production failure deterministically. Before: amber, "1 item needs confirmation". After, local and again on production: green, "the prefix strokes measure heavier than the warning body". **Rejected alternative**: swapping the demo image again — the image was never the problem, and three of the four candidates measured worse. **Also rejected**: padding the measurement crop or lowering the gate's resolution floor to make marginal cases resolve — the gate's zero-confident-mistakes evidence (rubric C9) is tied to that exact pre-processing, and loosening it to fix a demo card trades a real safety property for a cosmetic one.

**Method note.** The 4/4 live stability run that cleared this image earlier measured the WARNING VERDICT and the model's bold ADVISORY — not the client-side gate that actually decides the card. The instrument did not touch the thing being claimed, which is why it passed a card that then failed in front of the owner.

## 2026-08-13 (later still) — one verdict, one voice, and the demo cards that promised the wrong colour

**Owner walkthrough of the shipped build, six findings, all of them the same disease: two surfaces describing one label differently.**

**The headline, the top bar and the row now come from one tally.** `ResultView` counted the fields for its banner, `SingleCheck.outcomeSummary` counted them again for the stepper, and the second count knew nothing about the agent's decisions. Reject the bold type and the warning panel said FAIL while the banner said "1 item needs confirmation" and the top of the page said "Result: matched" — on a label the agent had just ruled non-compliant. Worse, when the measurement gate had verified bold and the agent overruled it, the banner read "Label matches the application". The tally moved into `lib/verdict.ts` (`summarizeVerdict`), which both surfaces render and which 13 tests pin. A rejected bold type is now what it always was in law: a failure of the government warning, red, named in the headline and in the top bar. **Rejected alternative**: teaching `outcomeSummary` about bold too — two counts that agree today drift apart at the next change, which is exactly how this one arose.

**A bold glance is owed, measuring, or resolved — never all three at once.** The banner used to print "1 review" and "1 to confirm (bold)" for the same single outstanding item, because one came from the confirm count and the other from a separate boolean. The gate's *running* state is now explicit (`boldMeasuring`), so the page says "checking bold type" while it measures instead of asking for a confirmation it is about to resolve on its own, and the item is counted once.

**The clean demo card asked for a bold confirmation.** `clean-match--rot2` preserves its verdict through the deskew (which is why the picker chose it) but the straightened letterforms are too soft for the stroke-width gate, which returned "human" — so the card whose entire claim is "everything lines up" came back amber. The picker now selects the clean card against `bold-gate-rescore.mjs` as well as the verdict matrix, **and runs the candidate live four times** before accepting it: the matrix is a single API call per image, and the model varies between runs. ## 2026-08-13 (later) — a second audit sweep, and the two blockers the first round's fixes introduced

**A fix round is new surface, and it was audited as such.** The second sweep pointed its code and security lanes at `d3bdf8e..HEAD` — the eight commits of audit fixes themselves — on the theory that documentation and code written to answer an auditor is exactly where fresh over-claiming lands. ## 2026-08-13 — deskew before the read, and demo samples that are photographs

**A pre-read deskew shipped** (`lib/enhance.ts`, wired into `prepareImage` in `lib/downscale.ts`). Skew is estimated by projection-profile variance over a ±20° sweep and corrected by an expanded bilinear rotation. **Measured on the 424 images enhancement actually modifies: confirmed-clean 13 → 31, false rejections 5 → 3, text-defect violations missed 1 → 0.** The evidence file's own top-level verdict says `DO_NOT_SHIP` (aggregate missed violations 3 → 6) and is deliberately left unedited — that aggregate lumps bold-only labels in with text defects. Built a 48-case matrix (`samples/tools/bold-densitometry-matrix.mjs`, results `docs/bold-densitometry-matrix.json`): 6 fonts × bold/regular, 3 type sizes, 3 resolutions, JPEG-q70, dark background, semibold-600 boundary, all-bold violation, and a COLA-typical combo (0.75× + JPEG q70 ≈ the recommended dpi band). **The method fails**: (1) no cross-font threshold exists — bold Times measures 1.45 while non-bold Georgia/Verdana measure 1.53 (font design contributes as much ink-density signal as weight); (2) at COLA-typical quality, 3 of 4 measurable bold prefixes read as NOT bold (1.26–1.38) — the same directional bias blur showed, triggered by TTB's own recommended submission format; (3) 10px type and 0.5× resolution are unmeasurable; (4) the all-bold violation case defeats the metric structurally (it is a ratio against a body reference assumed non-bold — circular when the body's boldness is itself in question); (5) it can't distinguish semibold from bold. The earlier 11/11 was an artifact of a single-font, sharp, uniform-resolution sample set. **Decision: no pixel-based bold auto-verify ships.** The AI stroke-weight judgment (16/17, robust to font/format/resolution in ways pixel heuristics are not) stays the advisory signal, human confirmation stays, and the right investment is making the human step cheap (batch bold spot-check strip, deferred roadmap). **Rejected alternatives**: shipping two-signal auto-verify on the 11-label evidence (would have auto-verified wrongly on Times-style serifs and auto-flagged clean bold labels submitted at TTB's own recommended quality); tuning per-font thresholds (requires font identification — heavier and more fragile than the AI judgment it would corroborate); validating against real COLA-registry label images (the public registry is a form-driven portal; noted as the next validation step if this is ever revisited).
